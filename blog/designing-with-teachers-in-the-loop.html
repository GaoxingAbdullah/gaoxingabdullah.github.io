Designing with Teachers in the Loop

Lessons from participatory studies in classroom analytics.

When we build AI tools for education, teachers are often treated as passive end-users — people who receive a dashboard and are expected to act on its recommendations. But what happens when we flip that dynamic and invite teachers to be co-designers from day one?

Why Participatory Design Matters

Classroom analytics systems frequently fail not because of technical shortcomings, but because they solve the wrong problems. A model might accurately predict which students are at risk of falling behind, yet the teacher already knows this — what they need is actionable guidance on how to intervene, given 35 students and a 45-minute lesson.

Participatory design (PD) addresses this gap by centering teachers' lived expertise throughout the development cycle. Instead of validating a finished prototype, PD involves educators in defining the problem space, sketching interface ideas, and iterating on working systems inside real classrooms.

Three Takeaways from Our Studies

1. Teachers value context over precision

In our workshops at two primary schools, teachers consistently preferred explanations that were contextual — "this student's engagement dropped after the group activity" — over abstract confidence scores. Building interpretability around classroom events, not model internals, led to far higher adoption in follow-up trials.

2. Timing is everything

A beautiful report delivered after the school day ends has little impact. Teachers told us they need micro-insights during transitions between activities — the 90-second windows when they can actually adjust their plan. This pushed us toward lightweight, glanceable notifications rather than comprehensive dashboards.

3. Co-design builds trust

Perhaps the most important finding: teachers who participated in design sessions were significantly more willing to trust and experiment with the system, even when predictions were imperfect. The process of being heard — seeing their feedback reflected in the next iteration — created a sense of ownership that no amount of accuracy metrics could replicate.

Looking Ahead

Our next phase involves scaling these methods to secondary schools in multilingual settings. The core question remains the same: how do we build AI that genuinely serves the people closest to the problem? Starting with their expertise — not our algorithms — seems like the most honest answer.
